## I. Before the Brothers

Thousands of years before the rivalry between Urza and
Mishra, Dominaria was home to one of the most technologically
advanced civilizations in its history: the <GlossaryTerm id="thran">Thran</GlossaryTerm> Empire.

The Thran built extraordinary machines and structures,
powered by magical crystals known as <GlossaryTerm id="powerstones">powerstones</GlossaryTerm>.

At the heart of their civilization stood Halcyon, a city
whose technological achievements reflected the wealth and
influence of the empire.

But the same technology that supported Thran society
would eventually contribute to its destruction.

## II. The Civilization of Powerstones

Much of the Thran Empire's technological supremacy rested
on the achievements of <CharacterLink id="glacian">Glacian</CharacterLink>,
an exceptionally gifted engineer and inventor.

He discovered how to create powerstones: magical crystals
capable of holding extraordinary quantities of energy.
Their development changed what Thran artificers and
architects could accomplish.

His development of powerstones allowed the Thran to store
and harness enormous quantities of energy.

Powerstones became fundamental to the empire's machinery,
architecture, and daily life.

Glacian's inventions transformed the Thran world.

Yet his achievements contained an unexpected danger.
Exposure to the powerstones was associated with a
devastating illness known as phthisis.

The civilization had become dependent on a technology
that could also threaten the lives of its people.

</HistoricalMoment>

Glacian's wife, Rebbec, was among the most celebrated
architects of the empire.

Her works included extraordinary structures that
combined engineering and artifice, culminating in the
magnificent Thran Temple above Halcyon.

Together, Glacian and <CharacterLink id="rebbec">Rebbec</CharacterLink>
represented two complementary forms of Thran ingenuity.
Glacian supplied the power that enabled the empire's
technological achievements; Rebbec transformed that
potential into monumental architecture.

Yet their accomplishments also exposed the contradiction
at the center of Thran civilization. The empire depended
on powerstones even as their effects contributed to
a devastating medical crisis.

Their lives would become inseparable from its fall.

## III. The Arrival of Yawgmoth

As sickness spread through Thran society, the empire
faced a crisis that its remarkable technology could
not easily resolve.

<CharacterLink id="rebbec">Rebbec</CharacterLink> sought treatment for her
ailing husband, <CharacterLink id="glacian">Glacian</CharacterLink>, and used
her influence to bring a physician named
<CharacterLink id="yawgmoth">Yawgmoth</CharacterLink> to Halcyon.

Glacian's condition provided Yawgmoth with access to
the very center of Thran society. What began as a search
for medical treatment helped introduce an ambitious
physician to the empire's most influential circles.

Yawgmoth was brilliant and ambitious.

His medical knowledge made him valuable to a society
desperate for a cure. But his methods and personal
ambitions would prove increasingly dangerous.

<HistoricalMoment cardId={frontmatter.cardIds[1]} significance={frontmatter.cards[1].significance}>

Yawgmoth gained influence through his apparent ability
to confront the suffering that afflicted the Thran.

His pursuit of medical progress became entangled with

The physician who presented himself as a potential
savior would eventually become the greatest threat
to the civilization that welcomed him.

</HistoricalMoment>

As his authority increased, Yawgmoth began to see
living beings as subjects that could be transformed
according to his own vision of perfection.

### How a physician became a political authority

Yawgmoth's arrival in Halcyon is best understood as a crisis of trust as well as a medical emergency. Glacian's condition made the powerstone disease impossible for the elite to ignore, yet the people whom Thran society had already pushed aside had lived with the consequences of its prosperity for far longer. Rebbec did not invite a conqueror into the capital. She brought in a physician who claimed that he could treat a sickness for which respectable Thran authorities had failed to provide an answer. That initial claim was powerful because it promised relief without forcing the empire to reconsider the conditions that had made so many people vulnerable.

Once Yawgmoth gained access to Halcyon's institutions, a difference emerged between *treating a patient* and *controlling a population*. Medical authority could determine who needed treatment, where they should be confined, and whose testimony counted as evidence. People labeled dangerous or diseased were easier to isolate. The Untouchables' grievances, which had both social and material causes, could be reframed as disorders to be managed rather than injustices to be addressed. In this reading, Yawgmoth's rise did not happen despite the Thran state's strengths. It exploited the state's ability to centralize decisions, resources, and coercion.

That distinction helps explain why individual goodwill could not save Halcyon. Rebbec sought a remedy for her husband, but she did not control the uses to which Yawgmoth put the position she helped him obtain. The tragedy is not that healing was an unworthy goal. It is that an emergency created an opening for someone willing to convert dependence into obedience.

## IV. The Discovery of Phyrexia

A planeswalker named Dyfed introduced Yawgmoth to
an artificial plane known as <GlossaryTerm id="phyrexia">Phyrexia</GlossaryTerm>.

The plane had been created by an earlier, unnamed
planeswalker and was no longer under its creator's
control.

Phyrexia contained a strange environment in which
artifice and living systems were deeply intertwined.

To Yawgmoth, it offered extraordinary possibilities.

He believed its properties could be used to confront
the disease afflicting the Thran, but the plane also
offered a place to conduct experiments far beyond
the restraints of his former society.

A portal established a connection between Dominaria
and Phyrexia.

Over time, Yawgmoth became the artificial plane's
new master.

Under his direction, it developed into the center
of a doctrine that sought to remake living beings
through a union of flesh, machinery, and magic.

## V. The Fall of the Thran Empire

The Thran Empire was already weakened by disease,
political division, and competing visions of its
future.

Yawgmoth's growing power made those divisions
increasingly destructive.

His experiments and use of Phyrexian forces
turned a crisis of medicine and government into
a catastrophe for the entire civilization.

Resistance to Yawgmoth escalated into war.

The empire's technological achievements, once
symbols of progress, became instruments of ruin.

Halcyon and the society surrounding it were
devastated.

What had once appeared to be the height of
Dominarian civilization came to an end.


### A civil war with more than two sides

The fall of the Thran cannot be reduced to the familiar shape of rebels overthrowing a cruel empire. Halcyon's privileged families, exploited laborers, ambitious officials, physicians, and soldiers entered the crisis with different expectations. Some hoped to preserve the city and its powerstone economy. Others wanted an end to exclusion. Yawgmoth offered apparent solutions to people with conflicting interests, then used the resulting access to reshape the conflict around his own authority. The name *Phyrexia* eventually gives this story a clear villainous destination, but the first steps toward catastrophe took place inside an existing human society.

The artificial plane beyond Dyfed's gateway changed the scale of the disaster. On Dominaria, Yawgmoth had to work through the Thran's institutions and face at least the possibility of resistance from those institutions. On Phyrexia he could pursue experiments and manufacture a political order increasingly defined by his own beliefs. Patients could become experimental subjects; bodies could be redesigned into instruments; loyalty could be demanded as a condition of survival. The history of his rule therefore connects the breakdown of Halcyon's civic life with the birth of a much more durable machinery of domination.

Not every person who feared the Thran elite supported what Yawgmoth became, and not every opponent of Yawgmoth had been a defender of the oppressed. Keeping those distinctions visible makes the historical conflict more credible. The social crisis made his ascent possible; it did not make his decisions inevitable.

## VII. The Legacy of Glacian

The Thran civilization disappeared, but some of
its artifacts endured long after its cities
had fallen into ruin.

Among the most important surviving objects were two
halves of a broken powerstone associated with Glacian.

Yawgmoth had implanted those halves into Glacian's body,
where they drained his life force. Rebbec later used
the stones to seal the gateway between Dominaria
and Phyrexia.

These were not merely relics of a vanished civilization.
They preserved a direct connection between Glacian's
engineering, Yawgmoth's cruelty, and Rebbec's final act.

Millennia later, the young brothers

<CharacterLink id="urza">Urza</CharacterLink> and Mishra discovered the stones
while exploring ancient Thran ruins.

They named the two halves the Mightstone and Weakstone.

The discoveries made by an engineer thousands of years
earlier would become central to a new struggle between
artificers, eventually contributing to the
Brothers' War.

Thus, the history of the Thran connects directly
to the beginning of the Brothers' War.


### Why the two powerstone halves mattered later

For Urza and Mishra, the stones recovered at Koilos were objects of discovery and rivalry long before either brother understood their ancient context. For a reader who already knows the Thran catastrophe, they are evidence that technological objects can survive the disappearance of every institution that once explained them. Glacian's legacy crossed thousands of years without carrying an equally complete warning about the environment, people, and politics of its creation.

That loss of context is a recurring feature of Dominaria's history. Future scholars can identify a machine's strength while misunderstanding the society that built it. A surviving device can be copied, weaponized, or treated as a prize. Even the language of progress becomes difficult to separate from archaeology: one era calls something an invention that an earlier civilization would have recognized as a dangerous inheritance. The Brothers' War repeats this pattern on an enormous scale when competition over artifacts overtakes the brothers' original curiosity.

The surviving pieces are not proof that the Thran intended Urza's war, or that Glacian knowingly set every later disaster in motion. Such claims would turn a long and contingent history into a single predetermined chain. Their historical significance lies in continuity without understanding. Objects endure, people die, and the meaning of the same power changes as it passes between generations.

## VIII. How Magic Told This Story

Although the Thran existed in Magic's distant
fictional past, their history was revealed to
readers gradually through later publications.

The novel _The Thran_, written by J. Robert King
and published in 1999, explored the empire's
decline and Yawgmoth's rise in detail.

Later card releases revisited major figures
from this period.

_Modern Horizons_ (2019) introduced
**Yawgmoth, Thran Physician**, depicting the
character before his later transformation.

_Commander Legends_ (2020) introduced cards
representing Glacian and Rebbec, providing
illustrations of two central figures from
this ancient civilization.

Earlier, _Urza's Destiny_ (1999) used the Thran name to evoke Urza's
later artifice. _The Brothers' War_ (2022) depicted Gix and the
Mightstone and Weakstone in their later story context. These are
retrospective illustrations, not a timeline of events in ancient Halcyon.

Together, these publications illustrate how
Magic's historical storytelling extends far
beyond the chronological order of its expansions.

The Thran may have vanished thousands of years
before the Brothers' War, but the consequences
of their civilization survived through its
technology, its artifacts, and the rise of Phyrexia.

## IX. Beneath Halcyon: The People the Empire Abandoned

The story of Halcyon cannot be understood solely through its rulers, its soaring temple, or the brilliance of its engineers. The Thran Empire also had an underclass: people pushed out of respectable public life and confined beneath the city in the **Caves of the Damned**. They were called the *Untouchables*, a name that turned illness and exclusion into a social identity. The machines above them still demanded powerstone production, and the people beneath those machines bore a disproportionate share of their cost.

Phthisis was not simply an inexplicable plague sweeping an innocent population. Exposure to the empire's powerstone technology lay behind the disease. That distinction matters: Halcyon's response treated suffering people as threats to be segregated rather than confronting the hazards built into its infrastructure. The hidden cost of Thran prosperity was neither distant nor accidental. It was directly connected to the systems that kept the capital functioning.

Among the outcasts was <CharacterLink id="gix">Gix</CharacterLink>. Long before his name belonged to a Phyrexian praetor, he was a human rebel furious at the inequality and sickness imposed on his people. In the novel's account, he wounded <CharacterLink id="glacian">Glacian</CharacterLink> with a powerstone fragment during the unrest at the Mana Rig. The attack did not undo the empire's injustice. Instead, the injured inventor became desperately ill, opening the way for Rebbec to summon the exiled physician Yawgmoth.

This is one of the story's most consequential ironies. A revolt against the harm caused by powerstones helped bring the man who would turn the Thran medical crisis into the foundation of Phyrexian rule. Gix did not begin as a machine-born monster; his later transformation is part of the tragedy, not a condition that should be projected backward onto his earlier life.

## X. Medicine, Public Safety, and the Capture of the State

<CharacterLink id="yawgmoth">Yawgmoth</CharacterLink> returned to Halcyon with the credentials of a remarkable healer and the reputation of a dangerous thinker. Rebbec's immediate purpose was personal: she wanted someone capable of treating her husband when other physicians had failed. Yawgmoth's opportunity was political. To deal with phthisis, he needed access to sick people, instruments, workers, and the city's governing institutions. Each concession made in the name of emergency care widened his authority.

He correctly connected powerstone exposure with the sickness and developed treatments that could relieve some of its effects. These achievements help explain why frightened citizens and influential officials entrusted him with further power. The danger lay not in a complete absence of medical skill but in the refusal to separate useful discoveries from coercive methods. The patients he claimed to protect became subjects over whom he could exercise control.

The physician could decide who was sick, who required confinement, and who deserved the treatment he controlled. His medical program blurred the distinction between the infected, the politically inconvenient, and those who questioned him. For the Untouchables, access to relief was never simply a matter of healing: it could be made conditional upon cooperation. For Halcyon's elite, the promise of restored order made extraordinary powers seem acceptable.

Gix's movement illustrates that trap. Yawgmoth offered treatment to a population whose rebellion challenged the established order, and in doing so gained leverage over the rebels themselves. A protest rooted in exclusion and industrial harm was gradually redirected toward dependence on the city's most ambitious new authority. The physician's ascent therefore cannot be told as a single coup; it was an accumulation of permissions, medical decisions, manipulated loyalties, and violence.

## XI. Dyfed and the Gate Beyond Dominaria

The planeswalker **Dyfed** changed the scale of Yawgmoth's ambitions. She showed him an artificial world unlike Dominaria, a place where constructed environments and living systems could be made to interact in extraordinary ways. The world already existed; Yawgmoth **did not create the original plane of Phyrexia**. Its previous creator, an unnamed planeswalker, was gone. Dyfed also established the interplanar passage connecting the Thran world to it.

That portal transformed a crisis confined to Halcyon into a catastrophe with consequences across millennia. Yawgmoth could remove patients from the sight of the Thran authorities, reshape their bodies in an artificial environment, and develop a society increasingly dependent upon his will. He described the work as treatment and improvement. In practice, his experiments crossed from caring for vulnerable people into remaking them without meaningful freedom to refuse.

The early Phyrexia described in official histories was an artificial plane of **nine nested spheres**. Its structure made it more than a distant hospital or fortress: it was an environment whose deeper regions reflected increasingly extreme combinations of machinery, necrotic magic, and cruelty. Over time, the language of overcoming sickness became a doctrine of compulsory perfection. Yawgmoth imagined himself not merely as a physician working within the world but as the authority by which living beings would be redesigned.

Dyfed's role should not be confused with consent to everything Yawgmoth later did. She opened the possibility of travel and access. His decisions about what to build there, whom to transform, and how to wield that transformation belonged to him. Distinguishing these roles prevents the origins of Phyrexia from collapsing into the misleading claim that Yawgmoth simply invented a plane and immediately filled it with his armies.

## XII. The War for Halcyon

Thran resistance did not take the shape of a simple contest between people loyal to one city. Yawgmoth's interventions split the empire, while the danger spreading from the gateway drew opposition from those who feared the changes he was imposing. The conflict described in *The Thran* eventually involved an alliance of peoples confronting the armies and machines under his command. The civil collapse of Halcyon and the rise of Phyrexia were linked phases of the same disaster.

The new Phyrexian forces were not only weapons manufactured elsewhere. They were tied to what had happened to the Thran's own sick, displaced, and captive populations. The horror was that a program publicly defended as a route to healing had become a means of manufacturing obedience. This is the historical bridge between the physician of Halcyon and the later Father of Machines whose followers would invade Dominaria in the Weatherlight era.

<CharacterLink id="rebbec">Rebbec</CharacterLink> stands at the moral center of this ending. Her public stature and genuine architectural gifts had helped make Halcyon splendid, and her efforts to save Glacian had helped Yawgmoth enter its center of power. When the empire could not be preserved, she faced a narrower but decisive choice: whether to leave a path open for Yawgmoth's forces to return from Phyrexia.

She sealed that passage at the cost of her own life. The act did not reverse the suffering, rescue the lost city, or erase the powerstones' history. It denied Phyrexia immediate access to Dominaria and made a later return a different, far more difficult undertaking. Rebbec's action belongs alongside her earlier mistakes because it represented a change in judgment under devastating conditions—not an effortless victory after which the world was safe.

<HistoricalEventReference id="thran-war-for-halcyon" />


### Reading Thran history backward without distorting it

Many players first encounter the Thran as an explanation for later names: Phyrexia, Yawgmoth, the Mightstone, the Weakstone, or an artifact in Urza's workshop. Reading backward is useful, but it creates a risk. It makes Halcyon's citizens appear important only because of what their descendants or enemies would do centuries afterward. For the people living beneath the Thran Temple, the immediate questions were already immense: who benefited from technology, who bore the cost of disease, and whether the powerful would recognize their responsibility to the vulnerable.

This is why Rebbec, Glacian, Gix, and Dyfed deserve attention apart from the fate of the stones. Their choices reveal different kinds of agency. Engineering could create both beauty and dependence; protest could emerge from injustice without guaranteeing a just outcome; planar travel could expose a new refuge or a new instrument of captivity. Rebbec's last action closed a gate, but it did not erase the harm that allowed the gate's ruler to rise.

The distinction between the historical era and the date of a card's publication is equally important. Later products provide portraits of people whose decisive actions happened long before the invention of the trading-card game. These illustrations can orient a reader and preserve recognizable identities, but an attractive retrospective image is not evidence that the depicted scene happened exactly as painted. Read the chapter's source material and each card's stated significance together rather than treating one as a substitute for the other.

## XIII. The Two Stones That Outlived an Empire

The most direct material link between this ancient history and the Brothers' War is a pair of powerstone halves. <CharacterLink id="glacian">Glacian</CharacterLink>'s fatal suffering, Yawgmoth's manipulations, and Rebbec's sealing of the gateway are all associated with the stones. Far from becoming harmless museum pieces, they remained bound to a history of invention and coercion.

Centuries passed into millennia. The empire that had produced the stones was no longer a living political order. Then <CharacterLink id="urza">Urza</CharacterLink> and <CharacterLink id="mishra">Mishra</CharacterLink> encountered the ancient artifacts in Thran ruins. Their discovery transformed the stones into the **Mightstone** and **Weakstone**, names that would become inseparable from the brothers' rivalry. Both men inherited something they only partly understood: astonishing technical power and the consequences of a civilization that had already been destroyed by its own ambitions.

The broader pattern is equally important. Phyrexia would exploit Mishra during the Brothers' War; centuries later, Urza and the Weatherlight crew would fight Yawgmoth's forces directly. Much later still, the rise of New Phyrexia would echo the language of improvement that had first been used to justify Yawgmoth's domination. Those are **separate episodes across distinct eras**, not a single uninterrupted war, but they share a historical origin in the Thran catastrophe.
