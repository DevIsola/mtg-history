## I. Beyond Dominaria's Horizon

Dominaria's struggles remain essential to the history of Magic, but they never encompassed the entire Multiverse. While planeswalkers confronted the temporal rifts that culminated in the Mending, independent civilizations elsewhere faced crises shaped by their own laws, landscapes, and beliefs. Their histories do not fit neatly into a single sequence of Dominarian dates.

This chapter follows three such worlds through their first major expansion-block stories: Ravnica's struggle to preserve a magical political order, Zendikar's transformation from a perilous frontier into a battlefield against the <GlossaryTerm id="eldrazi">Eldrazi</GlossaryTerm>, and Innistrad's desperate defense of humanity from the supernatural. These arcs form the groundwork for later convergences, but the battles of the War of the Spark and the newer Phyrexian invasion belong to separate chapters.

## II. Ravnica: A City Bound by Law

Ravnica was a world almost entirely covered by city. Its ten guilds were far more than clubs of mages: they managed law, policing, finance, biological research, transport, secrets, and a host of other functions on which ordinary citizens depended. Their power derived in part from the <GlossaryTerm id="guildpact">Guildpact</GlossaryTerm>, a magically binding settlement created thousands of years earlier to restrain open warfare among the guilds.

The settlement did not make Ravnica just or peaceful. It enforced a balance between competing institutions, many of which exploited people outside their ranks. The Azorius Senate defended legal order; Boros soldiers policed the streets; House Dimir operated in obscurity; the Orzhov made debt and obligation instruments of rule; and other guilds served similarly entangled civic and private interests.

The ten-thousandth anniversary of the Guildpact's founding—the Decamillennial—was supposed to mark the endurance of that arrangement. Instead, it exposed how thoroughly Ravnica's peace relied on old magic that few understood.


### What it meant to live without a guild

Ravnica's architecture makes its guilds appear inseparable from everyday life. The Azorius Senate helped define law, the Boros Legion enforced it, the Orzhov turned obligation into an economic system, and other guilds controlled infrastructure that most residents could not avoid. Yet the city was not composed exclusively of guild members. The unguilded had to find work, safety, and political influence in a place where major services and public decisions were dominated by institutions with their own magical privileges.

This is why the Guildpact's stability should not be confused with fairness. The agreement restrained open conflict between certain powers but also protected a system in which those powers could remain dominant for millennia. For a guild official, order might mean predictable jurisdiction. For a street vendor or tenant, it could mean being subject to rules whose authors were beyond reach. Agrus Kos's investigation matters because it exposed cracks in a legal order that most citizens were expected to treat as permanent.

The city's history also shows how useful a rigid law can be to someone who understands its exceptions. Szadek did not need to defeat every guild in open battle in order to create a constitutional crisis. He needed to maneuver events so that enforcement itself undermined the binding settlement. The paradox is more interesting than a simple plot to seize a throne: a system built to guarantee order could be broken by using the system's own procedures.

## III. Agrus Kos and the Hidden Guild

<CharacterLink id="agrus-kos">Agrus Kos</CharacterLink>, an experienced wojek investigator of the Boros Legion, pursued a conspiracy whose clues cut across the city's institutional boundaries. The case led toward <CharacterLink id="szadek">Szadek</CharacterLink>, a psychic vampire who founded House Dimir and remained hidden behind its agents and false narratives.

The investigation captured more than a single criminal plot. It showed how a city bound by magical rules could be manipulated by those who knew exactly which acts those rules permitted and punished. The very effort to expose and arrest Szadek helped bring down the existing Guildpact, fulfilling a larger design in which Azorius manipulation also played a part.

## IV. The Broken Guildpact and New Ambitions

With the foundational pact no longer restraining the guilds in the same way, old rivalries could grow into more direct contests for power. <CharacterLink id="teysa-karlov">Teysa Karlov</CharacterLink>, a resourceful figure in the Orzhov Syndicate, navigated the aftermath through bargains, political intelligence, and uneasy cooperation with Agrus Kos.

The Simic Combine's Momir Vig pursued another form of dominance through biological engineering. His Project Kraj created a threat that brought the city's conflicts to a violent climax, while the demon Rakdos became entangled in the confrontation. The collapse of the Guildpact thus gave way not to a single victorious guild, but to a dangerous reordering of the whole city.

<HistoricalMoment cardId={frontmatter.cardIds[2]} significance={frontmatter.cards[2].significance}>

</HistoricalMoment>

<HistoricalEventReference id="ravnica-project-kraj" />

### Replacing a magical law with a negotiated settlement

The loss of the first Guildpact was not followed by an immediate replacement with equal supernatural force. After the struggles involving Project Kraj and the confrontation at Prahv, <CharacterLink id="teysa-karlov">Teysa Karlov</CharacterLink> drafted a new, nonmagical accord. Its promise was political: the surviving guilds could negotiate a basis for cooperation without pretending that the old enchantment had survived. The official Ravnica retrospective describes this as a fragile settlement rather than a new ten-thousand-year guarantee.

The distinction between a magically compulsory constitution and an ordinary agreement changes how we understand the city's recovery. A written accord cannot compel the ambitions of guildmasters, prevent opportunistic violence, or return destroyed institutions to service. Teysa's work belongs after the collapse and its subsequent crises; it should not be confused with the much later **Living Guildpact**, or with a single final victory over House Dimir.

## V. Ravnica's Place in the Wider Crisis

Ravnica's events did not require its inhabitants to understand Dominaria's history. Yet the Multiverse was not a collection of completely isolated systems. The strange accumulation of spirits in the ghost quarter Agyrem was related to damage in the planar fabric and to Ravnica's temporary isolation during Dominaria's temporal crisis.

Ravnica would later be home to planeswalker Jace Beleren and to a renewed form of the Guildpact. These developments belong to subsequent arcs; they do not retroactively make Jace the protagonist of the original Decamillennial narrative. For this chapter, the lasting question is what happens when law itself becomes a mechanism of intrigue.


### The political aftermath of a magical contract

The original Guildpact's destruction did not leave an immediate, uncomplicated democracy. Guilds still possessed supporters, money, soldiers, debts, and institutions. The disappearance of magical limits meant that old forms of competition could become more overt and that new claimants could seek power. Teysa Karlov's role in later negotiations illustrates a different response from the conspirators: the use of legal expertise to construct a political settlement when the previous settlement's supernatural guarantee had failed.

The shift matters beyond Ravnica. It distinguishes a world where magic *enforces* an agreement from a world where people must continually persuade one another to honor it. Such a distinction recurs throughout the Multiverse: a spell can preserve a boundary or impose a peace, but it cannot make every participant trustworthy. Ravnica's next historical period would revisit the problem under new forms of the Guildpact, including its eventual embodiment in a living person. Those later developments should not be projected backward onto Agrus Kos's original investigation.

The first Ravnica block was told in its own publication context. The later War of the Spark and Phyrexian invasion show the same city's districts during different crises. Keeping those stories separate allows returning names and guild symbols to carry history rather than erase it.

## VI. Zendikar: A Living, Dangerous World

Zendikar's landscapes were known for ruin, peril, and sudden transformation. Shifting terrain and ferocious elemental upheavals—the Roil—made exploration dangerous even without an enemy army. Adventurers sought relics among ancient stone formations whose original function had been forgotten.

Those formations were not monuments made merely for treasure-seekers. The floating hedrons were components of a prison. Long before the events of the _Zendikar_ block, three planeswalkers had confronted the Eldrazi: <CharacterLink id="nahiri">Nahiri</CharacterLink>, a lithomancer native to Zendikar; Sorin Markov; and the spirit dragon Ugin.

The trio could not simply destroy the Eldrazi titans, so they lured them to Zendikar and contained them through a lattice of leylines and hedrons. Ugin devised the prison's design, and Nahiri spent decades shaping its stone machinery. Sorin helped secure the trap. The extraordinary scheme protected other worlds by placing an existential danger within Nahiri's own home.

<HistoricalMoment cardId={frontmatter.cardIds[3]} significance={frontmatter.cards[3].significance}>

</HistoricalMoment>

### The decision that placed a prison on Nahiri's home world

Ugin's proposal was not merely an engineering challenge. He wanted to bind the Eldrazi to one mana-rich world, avoiding the uncertain consequences of trying to destroy them. As the original story *The Lithomancer* explains, <CharacterLink id="nahiri">Nahiri</CharacterLink> confronted the terrible moral cost of choosing Zendikar: its people and ecosystems would carry the danger so that other planes could be spared. <CharacterLink id="sorin">Sorin</CharacterLink> and Ugin could help create the confinement, but Nahiri would remain to watch over it.

Her decision preceded the decades of lithomancy that produced the hedrons. Consent under such conditions was neither a celebration of the Eldrazi nor proof that Zendikar's inhabitants had been consulted. It was a choice made under a threat already devastating other worlds. Later explorers could see the prison's stones without knowing why their plane had been selected.

## VII. The Eye of Ugin and the Breaking of the Seal

The Eye of Ugin was a focus of the prison. In later events on Zendikar, Jace Beleren and Chandra Nalaar, while drawn into machinations involving Nicol Bolas and Sarkhan Vol, became involved in a clash that damaged the Eye's protective magic. The consequences were immediate: the ancient confinement weakened, and Eldrazi spawn began to spread.

The distinction matters. The Eye's disturbance did not originate with all three Eldrazi titans marching openly across Zendikar in a single instant. Rather, weakening the seals set in motion a larger catastrophe, and the plane's inhabitants had to understand an enemy they scarcely recognized.

### Hedrons, ruins, and the danger of mistaking a prison for a treasure

Zendikar rewarded exploration with extraordinary discoveries: structures suspended above the landscape, unfamiliar artifacts, and places where the environment itself seemed to resist settlement. A traveler could reasonably interpret these as signs of a lost civilization or as opportunities for wealth. The much older purpose of the hedrons was far less reassuring. They formed part of a system created to restrain beings whose existence exceeded ordinary ideas of monsters and armies.

That mismatch between appearance and purpose is essential to the tragedy. A prison can remain effective long after the people who built it vanish from public memory, but its security becomes fragile when new inhabitants do not know what they are preserving. The Eye of Ugin was not simply a dungeon containing a useful prize. It was bound up with the ancient effort of Nahiri, Sorin, and Ugin to contain the Eldrazi. Later disturbances of that system did not create the titans; they weakened restraints that had held them at a distance.

Zendikar's living landscape complicates the distinction between natural and artificial danger. Its violent terrain was not proof that its inhabitants deserved destruction, nor did every adventurer share responsibility for the eventual release. The Eldrazi story turns the romance of exploration inside out: discovery becomes perilous when knowledge of an object's function is missing and when the cost of a mistake falls on an entire world.

## VIII. The Eldrazi Emerge

Eldrazi were not merely large beasts living underground. Their titans were vast, alien entities whose relationship to ordinary physical reality was poorly understood by the mortals who fought their spawn. As the wards failed, Zendikar's communities confronted swarms and eventually titanic manifestations of an otherworldly hunger.

## IX. Innistrad: Humanity in the Shadow of Monsters

On Innistrad, the supernatural was not hidden at the margins. Vampires maintained bloodlines and estates, werewolves stalked the forests, necromancers disturbed graves, and restless spirits made death an uncertain boundary. Human settlements depended on cathars, local defenses, and the protections of the Church of Avacyn.

The church's angel was not an ancient deity. <CharacterLink id="sorin">Sorin Markov</CharacterLink>, an old vampire planeswalker, created <CharacterLink id="avacyn">Avacyn</CharacterLink> to stop the collapse of a terrible balance. If vampires preyed without restraint, humanity would eventually vanish—and the vampires' survival would be endangered as well.

<HistoricalMoment cardId={frontmatter.cardIds[7]} significance={frontmatter.cards[7].significance}>

_Sorin, Lord of Innistrad_ places its creator at the center of the plane's history. Sorin's protection was pragmatic as well as personal: he sought an enduring order in which humanity could survive without eliminating Innistrad's other inhabitants.

</HistoricalMoment>

<HistoricalEventReference id="innistrad-avacyn-created" />


### Innistrad's church defended more than a doctrine

Innistrad's villages lived under pressure from predators whose existence was not in dispute. Vampires, werewolves, spirits, and demons threatened travel, agriculture, burial practices, and the ability of families to remain in a place from generation to generation. In those circumstances, religious institutions did practical work. Rites and protections offered a way to organize fear, recognize communal obligations, and preserve some form of daily routine.

Avacyn's creation was an intervention in that fragile balance. Sorin did not make an angel simply to reward human piety. He understood that the unchecked destruction of humanity would eventually threaten the survival of the vampire population that depended on it. Avacyn's protection therefore had consequences beyond the church itself, because it helped keep predator and prey from destroying the world on which both lived.

When Griselbrand and Avacyn became trapped in the Helvault, people did not immediately learn the complete truth. They experienced weakened wards, the apparent absence of their protector, and a collapse of confidence in practices that had previously offered real security. The crisis was theological and material at the same time. Liliana's later involvement and the Helvault's destruction resolved one layer of danger while opening others. As on Ravnica and Zendikar, the visible heroic act makes sense only when the institutions and costs surrounding it are understood.

## X. Griselbrand and the Empty Throne

The demon <CharacterLink id="griselbrand">Griselbrand</CharacterLink> confronted Avacyn and tricked her into entering the Helvault, a magical prison she used to confine demons. Both were sealed away. With Avacyn absent, the church's wards and blessings weakened, and the horrors beyond the villages grew bolder. A crisis of faith accompanied the increasingly physical struggle for survival.

## XI. Liliana's Hunt and the Return of Avacyn

Liliana Vess arrived with her own purpose. She owed terrible debts to demons, and Griselbrand was one of the creditors she intended to eliminate. The Chain Veil gave her new power, but it also exacted a corrupting cost. Her search brought her into contact with the Helvault's secrets and with the human defenders desperate for Avacyn's return.

When the Helvault was shattered, the imprisonment ended: Avacyn emerged, but the confined demons were released as well. Thalia, a cathar, faced the consequences of the prison's destruction while Liliana pursued Griselbrand. The angel's reappearance restored hope and reinvigorated the struggle against Innistrad's darkness.

### The cathar's coerced choice at the Helvault

The Helvault did not simply burst open because a hero discovered a convenient release mechanism. The later official account *The Lunarch Inquisition* identifies a confrontation in Thraben Cathedral: Liliana threatened the lives of the soldiers under the cathar Thalia's command until Thalia yielded and broke the prison. Liliana's aim was access to <CharacterLink id="griselbrand">Griselbrand</CharacterLink>, one of the demons holding her to a bargain, not the restoration of the Avacynian Church.

The consequences exceeded either woman's immediate intentions. <CharacterLink id="avacyn">Avacyn</CharacterLink> returned, along with the imprisoned demons. The original *Helvault* artifact card represents the prison and its release mechanic; it does **not** illustrate the specific confrontation between Thalia and Liliana. Nor should Thalia's coerced act be portrayed as a freely chosen plan to release demons. The following restoration is a distinct historical consequence, not a second breaking of the same prison.

## XII. Three Planes, Three Forms of Power

Ravnica's Guildpact imposed power through rules and institutions. Zendikar's hedrons attempted to contain an incomprehensible threat through magical engineering. Avacyn embodied protection invested in a single extraordinary guardian. Each system preserved lives; each proved vulnerable when its central guarantee failed.

These worlds were also stages for an emerging generation of planeswalkers. After the Mending, individual travelers could participate directly in these conflicts without possessing the near-unlimited powers of Urza's generation. But the worlds and their people were never merely backdrops for those travelers. Agrus, Teysa, Nahiri, Avacyn, and Innistrad's human defenders acted within political and cultural histories that preceded any visit from Jace or Liliana.

The wider arcs would eventually intersect: Ravnica's guild politics would become crucial in the War of the Spark; Zendikar's Eldrazi crisis would reverberate beyond its plane; and Innistrad would face the consequences of an enemy whose imprisonment had begun elsewhere. Those are connected histories, not reasons to erase the boundaries between these first stories.


### Three histories that should not be forced into one chronology

Ravnica's original Guildpact crisis, Zendikar's first Eldrazi catastrophe, and Innistrad's Helvault story share a chapter because they show the breadth of the Multiverse, not because a single army moved from one to the next. Their local calendars, political actors, and narrative priorities differ. Some important Zendikar events concern a prison built far earlier than the expansion-block storyline; much of Innistrad's history begins with arrangements established long before the player meets its central characters.

Putting the arcs beside one another reveals a productive comparison. On Ravnica, people were bound by an agreement they no longer fully understood. On Zendikar, explorers encountered a protective structure whose original purpose they had forgotten. On Innistrad, communities relied on a defender whose disappearance they could not explain. In all three cases the past exerted power through institutions, landscapes, or symbols whose meaning had become incomplete.

That comparison is editorial, not an assertion that the settings are interchangeable. Their differences matter: Ravnica's threat began within its political institutions; Zendikar's was cosmic and ecological; Innistrad's put daily survival and faith under pressure. The later events that connect these planes are easier to understand when each retains its own history.

## XIII. How the Stories Were Published

Publication chronology is not story chronology. _Ravnica: City of Guilds_ (2005), _Guildpact_ (2006), and _Dissension_ (2006) established the guild world before _Time Spiral_ reached its ending in 2007. This does not mean Ravnica's events occurred after Dominaria's Mending. The original Ravnica trilogy was supported by novels by Cory J. Herndon and later official summaries that explain the sequence of the Guildpact's collapse.

_Zendikar_ (2009) and _Worldwake_ (2010) initially presented danger and exploration, with the Eye of Ugin warning of something much older. _Rise of the Eldrazi_ (2010) revealed the titans. Later Magic Story publications, especially _The Lithomancer_ and retrospective Zendikar summaries, supplied vital details about Nahiri, Sorin, Ugin, and the formation of the prison.

_Innistrad_ (2011), _Dark Ascension_ (2012), and _Avacyn Restored_ (2012) followed a different arc, from collapsing protection to the breaking of the Helvault and the angel's return. Later stories would revisit both the plane and its major figures, but those continuations should not be confused with the first block's conclusion.

For this archive, the expansion of the Multiverse means telling each world's history on its own terms—and tracing their intersections only where the sources support them.
